# Linear Algebra: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors
---

## 1. Fundamentals of Matrices & Linear Transformations

A matrix can be interpreted geometrically as a **linear transformation** that maps vectors from an input space to an output space. For example, a $2 \times 2$ matrix transforms a 2-dimensional vector into another 2-dimensional vector:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

In general terms, a transformation matrix shifts, rotates, or scales vectors across a coordinate grid.

---

## 2. Geometric Intuition of Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

When a matrix $A$ transforms a vector space, most vectors change their direction. However, certain special vectors remain along the exact same line (span) that they occupied before the transformation occurred. 

* **Eigenvector ($\vec{v}$):** A non-zero vector whose direction does **not** change when a linear transformation is applied to it.
* **Eigenvalue ($\lambda$):** The scalar factor by which the eigenvector scales (stretches, shrinks, or reverses) during the transformation.

### The Core Equation
$$A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$$

Where:
* $A$ is the transformation matrix.
* $\vec{v}$ is the eigenvector.
* $\lambda$ is the eigenvalue (a scalar constant).

> **Intuitive Insight:** Think of eigenvectors as the *axes of rotation* or the stable directions of a transformation matrix.

---

## 3. Mathematical Derivation & Calculation

To find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a square matrix $A$, we solve the characteristic equation:

$$A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$$
$$A\vec{v} - \lambda I\vec{v} = \vec{0}$$
$$(A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}$$

Where $I$ is the identity matrix. For a non-zero vector $\vec{v}$ to exist as a solution, the matrix $(A - \lambda I)$ must be **non-invertible** (singular). Therefore, its determinant must be zero:

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$$

### Complete Walkthrough Example
Given the matrix:
$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

#### Step 1: Find the Eigenvalues ($\lambda$)
Set up the characteristic determinant equal to zero:
$$\det\left( \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} \lambda & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda \end{bmatrix} \right) = 0$$

$$\det\begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda & 3 \\ 0 & 1-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = 0$$

Evaluate the determinant:
$$(2-\lambda)(1-\lambda) - (3 \times 0) = 0$$
$$\lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 2 = 0$$

Solving the quadratic equation gives the roots (Eigenvalues):
$$\lambda_1 = 2, \quad \lambda_2 = 1$$

---

#### Step 2: Find the Eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 2$
Substitute $\lambda = 2$ into $(A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}$:
$$\left(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\right) \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

This yields the following equations:
1. $0x_1 + 3x_2 = 0 \implies x_2 = 0$
2. $0x_1 - x_2 = 0 \implies x_2 = 0$

Since $x_1$ can be any free variable, we can choose $x_1 = 1$. Thus, the first basis eigenvector is:
$$\vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

---

#### Step 3: Find the Eigenvector for $\lambda_2 = 1$
Substitute $\lambda = 1$ into $(A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}$:
$$\left(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

This yields the linear relationship:
$$x_1 + 3x_2 = 0 \implies x_1 = -3x_2$$

Choosing $x_2 = 1$ gives $x_1 = -3$. Thus, the second basis eigenvector is:
$$\vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

#### Verification:
$$A\vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -6+3 \\ 0+1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1 \cdot \vec{v}_2 \quad \text{(Verified!)}$$

## 4. Key Properties of Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

1. **Sum of Eigenvalues (Trace):** The sum of all the eigenvalues of a matrix is equal to its trace (the sum of the diagonal elements of the matrix). This holds true regardless of whether the matrix is square or not.
2. **Product of Eigenvalues (Determinant):** The product of all the eigenvalues of a matrix is equal to its determinant. This also holds for square matrices.
3. **Orthogonality in Symmetric Matrices:** If a matrix $A$ is symmetric (i.e., $A = A^T$), the eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal to each other.
4. **Eigenvalue of an Identity Matrix:** For an identity matrix, the eigenvalues are all 1, regardless of the dimension of the matrix.
5. **Eigenvalue of a Scalar Multiple:** If $B$ is a matrix obtained by multiplying a scalar $c$ to a matrix $A$ (i.e., $B = cA$), then the eigenvalues of $B$ are just the eigenvalues of $A$ each multiplied by $c$.
6. **Eigenvalues of a Diagonal Matrix:** For a diagonal matrix, the eigenvalues are the diagonal elements themselves.
7. **Eigenvalues of a Transposed Matrix:** The eigenvalues of a matrix and its transpose are the same.

## 5. Real-World Application: Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Eigenvectors and eigenvalues play a critical role in data science, specifically in **Dimensionality Reduction** and **Feature Extraction** via PCA.

Imagine a dataset containing highly correlated features like student data (`CGPA`, `IQ`):

```text
   [ Data Features ] ---> [ Construct Covariance Matrix ] ---> [ Compute Eigenvectors ]
    (e.g., CGPA, IQ)                 (2 x 2)                     & Max Eigenvalues
```

## 6. Mechanism in PCA:
- The Covariance Matrix acts as a structured summary of the variances and dependencies (Var, Cov) between features.
- Because a covariance matrix is always symmetric, its derived eigenvectors are guaranteed to be completely orthogonal to each other.
- The resulting eigenvectors designate the directions of the new feature axes (Principal Components).
- The eigenvalues quantify the amount of data variance captured along each axis. Sorting by the maximum eigenvalue allows us to retain components that capture the maximum variance while discarding redundant dimensions.
