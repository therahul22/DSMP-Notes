## 1. Sparsity of Support Vectors & The $i \times j$ Grid

### 1.1 Why Dual Formulation Enables Kernels
Recall the Dual Optimization problem for SVM:

$$\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j (\mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_j) \right) \quad \text{s.t. } \alpha_i \ge 0, \quad \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i y_i = 0$$

Data points enter this equation **exclusively as dot products $(\mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_j)$**.

### 1.2 Sparsity of Alpha ($\alpha_i$)
By KKT Complementary Slackness:
* **Non-Support Vectors:** $\alpha_i = 0$ (They contribute zero to the optimization).
* **Support Vectors (SVs):** $\alpha_i > 0$ (Only these points actively define the decision boundary).

### 1.3 The $i \times j$ Interaction Grid
If a dataset has thousands of points but only $5$ support vectors ($1, 2, 3, 4, 5$), the double summation $\sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n}$ shrinks from a huge matrix down to a $5 \times 5$ grid of pairwise dot products:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 
(\mathbf{x}_1 \cdot \mathbf{x}_1) & (\mathbf{x}_1 \cdot \mathbf{x}_2) & (\mathbf{x}_1 \cdot \mathbf{x}_3) & (\mathbf{x}_1 \cdot \mathbf{x}_4) & (\mathbf{x}_1 \cdot \mathbf{x}_5) \\ 
(\mathbf{x}_2 \cdot \mathbf{x}_1) & (\mathbf{x}_2 \cdot \mathbf{x}_2) & (\mathbf{x}_2 \cdot \mathbf{x}_3) & (\mathbf{x}_2 \cdot \mathbf{x}_4) & (\mathbf{x}_2 \cdot \mathbf{x}_5) \\ 
(\mathbf{x}_3 \cdot \mathbf{x}_1) & (\mathbf{x}_3 \cdot \mathbf{x}_2) & (\mathbf{x}_3 \cdot \mathbf{x}_3) & (\mathbf{x}_3 \cdot \mathbf{x}_4) & (\mathbf{x}_3 \cdot \mathbf{x}_5) \\ 
(\mathbf{x}_4 \cdot \mathbf{x}_1) & (\mathbf{x}_4 \cdot \mathbf{x}_2) & (\mathbf{x}_4 \cdot \mathbf{x}_3) & (\mathbf{x}_4 \cdot \mathbf{x}_4) & (\mathbf{x}_4 \cdot \mathbf{x}_5) \\ 
(\mathbf{x}_5 \cdot \mathbf{x}_1) & (\mathbf{x}_5 \cdot \mathbf{x}_2) & (\mathbf{x}_5 \cdot \mathbf{x}_3) & (\mathbf{x}_5 \cdot \mathbf{x}_4) & (\mathbf{x}_5 \cdot \mathbf{x}_5)  
\end{pmatrix}$$

## 2. The Similarity Perspective & Kernel Substitution

### 2.1 Dot Product as Cosine Similarity
Cosine similarity between normalized vectors ($\|\mathbf{A}\| = 1, \|\mathbf{B}\| = 1$) is simply their dot product:

$$\text{Cosine Similarity} = \frac{\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}}{\|\mathbf{A}\| \|\mathbf{B}\|} = \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}$$

Thus, $(\mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_j)$ is fundamentally a **similarity score** between two support vectors.

### 2.2 Generalized Kernel Substitution
Replacing the standard dot product $(\mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_j)$ with a generalized similarity kernel $K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)$ yields the **Kernel SVM Dual Formulation**:

$$\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) \right)$$

## 3. Polynomial Kernel Mechanics & The Kernel Trick

### 3.1 Mechanics of the Kernel Trick
> **Crucial Reality:** The Kernel Trick **never** physically transforms data into a higher-dimensional space. All calculations are performed in the original lower-dimensional space.

### 3.2 Polynomial Kernel Expansion ($d = 2, \gamma = 1$)
For two 2D points $\mathbf{x}_i = (X_{11}, X_{12})$ and $\mathbf{x}_j = (X_{21}, X_{22})$:

$$K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = (1 + \mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_j)^2 = (1 + X_{11}X_{21} + X_{12}X_{22})^2$$

Expanding this gives a 6-term polynomial expression:
$$= 1 + X_{11}^2 X_{21}^2 + X_{12}^2 X_{22}^2 + 2X_{11}X_{21} + 2X_{12}X_{22} + 2X_{11}X_{21}X_{12}X_{22}$$

This matches the exact dot product of two transformed 6D vectors $\mathbf{x}_i' \cdot \mathbf{x}_j'$:

$$\mathbf{x}_i' = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & X_{11}^2 & X_{12}^2 & \sqrt{2}X_{11} & \sqrt{2}X_{12} & \sqrt{2}X_{11}X_{12} \end{bmatrix}$$
$$\mathbf{x}_j' = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & X_{21}^2 & X_{22}^2 & \sqrt{2}X_{21} & \sqrt{2}X_{22} & \sqrt{2}X_{21}X_{22} \end{bmatrix}$$

### 3.3 Path Comparison & Memory Advantage
1. **Path 1 (Explicit Mapping):** $\mathbf{x}_i \to \text{transform to } 6\text{D} \to \text{store in memory} \to \text{dot product}$.
2. **Path 2 (Kernel Trick):** Compute $(1 + \mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_j)^2$ directly in 2D space.

**Why Path 2 is Superior:** It avoids expanding memory consumption and computing expensive dot products in high dimensions.

### 3.4 Role of Cross-Interaction Terms ($X_1 X_2$)
* **Pure Squared Terms ($X_1^2, X_2^2$):** Create axis-aligned circular/elliptical boundaries.
* **Cross-Terms ($X_1 X_2$):** Provide flexibility to model **rotated/tilted ellipses, hyperbolas, and parabolas**.

## 4. Radial Basis Function (RBF) Kernel

### 4.1 Properties & Formula
RBF is the most popular, powerful, "best out-of-the-box" general-purpose kernel because it has no strong assumptions and satisfies the **Universal Approximation Property**.

Derived from the Normal (Gaussian) distribution formula $f(x) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$:

$$K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \exp\left( -\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) = \exp\left( -\gamma \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2 \right)$$

where $\gamma = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}$.

### 4.2 Distance vs. Similarity (Bounded $0$ to $1$)
* **Distance $= 0 \implies K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = e^0 = 1$** (Maximum similarity).
* **Distance is Large $\implies K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) \to 0$** (Zero similarity/influence).

### 4.3 Effect of Gamma ($\gamma$) & Sigma ($\sigma$)

$$\gamma \propto \frac{1}{\sigma^2}$$

* **High $\sigma$ (Low $\gamma$):** Flat bell curve $\to$ **Wide radius of influence** $\to$ Smoother boundary $\to$ **High Bias, Low Variance (Underfitting)**.
* **Low $\sigma$ (High $\gamma$):** Narrow spike bell curve $\to$ **Tight radius of influence** $\to$ Complex, local island boundaries $\to$ **Low Bias, High Variance (Overfitting)**.

## 5. Custom Kernels Overview

Custom kernels adapt SVMs to specialized, non-tabular domain data by defining custom similarity functions $K(x, y)$:

1. **String Kernels:** Measures similarity via shared substrings/subsequences in text or DNA sequences.
2. **Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) Kernel:** $K(x, y) = \exp(-\gamma \chi^2(x, y))$; used for comparing histogram distributions in Computer Vision.
3. **Intersection Kernel:** $K(x, y) = \sum \min(x_i, y_i)$; calculates overlap between histogram feature vectors.
4. **Hellinger's (Bhattacharyya) Kernel:** $K(x, y) = \sum \sqrt{x_i y_i}$; compares discrete probability distributions.
5. **RBFN Kernels:** RBF networks where center positions and widths ($\sigma_i$) are dynamically learned from data instead of fixed globally.
6. **Spectral Kernels:** Uses frequency/spectral transformations to analyze cyclic and periodic signals.
