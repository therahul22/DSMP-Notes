# Regression Analysis: Complete Study Notes

## 1. The Core Philosophy: Why Regression Analysis?

### Machine Learning vs. Real-World Statistics
In machine learning, we often focus strictly on taking sample data, training a model, and predicting an outcome. However, in real life, your training data is just a small **sample**. The ultimate goal is to understand how your model performs on the entire **population**. 

That is why we need statistical methods. They help us evaluate how reliable our sample predictions are and minimize **reducible error**—the error we can actually fix by building a better model, as opposed to irreducible random noise ($\epsilon$).

### Prediction vs. Inference
There is a fundamental trade-off between a model's predictive power and its interpretability:

* **Prediction:** Focuses entirely on forecasting the target outcome ($Y$) given inputs ($X$)—answering *"What will the salary/LPA be?"*. Here, highly complex models (like non-parametric or "black-box" models) are common because we care more about accuracy than how the model gets the answer.
* **Inference:** Focuses on understanding the *mathematical relationship* between variables—answering *"How does a change in CGPA or IQ affect salary?"*. For inference, simple **parametric models** (like Linear Regression) are highly preferred because their equations directly show the individual impact and strength of each feature.

---

## 2. Setting Up the Linear Regression Model

Regression analysis maps a dependent variable ($Y$) to one or more independent variables ($X$). 

### The Population Regression Function (True Relation)
$$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \dots + \beta_k X_k + \epsilon$$

* $\beta_0$: Intercept (the baseline value of $Y$ when all inputs are 0).
* $\beta_1, \beta_2, \dots, \beta_k$: Population coefficients (the true, hidden weights of the features).
* $\epsilon$: **Irreducible Error** (inherent random noise in the real world that no model can ever capture).

### The Estimated Regression Function (Your Model)
Because we don't have the whole population, we use our sample data to calculate estimates ($b_0$ and $b_i$, or $\hat{\beta}_0$ and $\hat{\beta}_i$):
$$\hat{Y} = b_0 + b_1 X_1 + b_2 X_2 + \dots + b_k X_k$$

The Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) method fits this line/plane by calculating estimates that **minimize the sum of squared residuals** (the gaps between true values and your predictions).

---

## 3. Decomposing Variance: TSS, RSS, and ESS

To measure how well our model fits, we break down the total variation of our data into three components:



### I. TSS (Total Sum of Squares)
The overall, raw variance present in your data before you even try to build a model. It measures how much the actual data points ($y_i$) deviate from their simple average ($\bar{y}$).
$$TSS = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2$$

### II. RSS (Residual Sum of Squares)
The unexplained variance. This measures the vertical distance between the actual data points ($y_i$) and the model's predictions ($\hat{y}_i$). High RSS means a poor fit.
$$RSS = \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

### III. ESS (Explained Sum of Squares)
The variance captured and successfully explained by your regression model. It tracks how much your line's predictions ($\hat{y}_i$) deviate from the baseline average ($\bar{y}$).
$$ESS = \sum (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2$$

### The Core Identity
$$TSS = ESS + RSS$$
$$\text{Total Variance} = \text{Explained Variance} + \text{Unexplained Variance}$$

---

## 4. Understanding Degrees of Freedom (DoF)

Degrees of Freedom represent the number of independent pieces of information left to estimate variability. In regression, the total DoF is split between the model and the errors:

* **Total DoF ($df_{\text{total}}$):** $n - 1$ (where $n$ is the number of observations/rows). We lose 1 degree of freedom because calculating the baseline sample mean $\bar{y}$ fixes the last data point.
* **Model DoF ($df_{\text{model}}$):** $k$ (where $k$ is the number of independent variables/columns).
* **Residual DoF ($df_{\text{residual}}$):** $n - k - 1$ (the remaining freedom after accounting for the intercept and all coefficients).

> 💡 **The Intuition (Geometric Minimums):**
> * To define a **line** ($k=1$), you need a minimum of 2 points. If you have exactly 2 points, your line fits them perfectly, leaving 0 room for error variability ($n - k - 1 \rightarrow 2 - 1 - 1 = 0$). You need at least $n=3$ points to get $df_{\text{residual}} = 1$.
> * To define a flat **plane** ($k=2$), you need a minimum of 3 points. Thus, you need at least $n=4$ points to get a residual degree of freedom of 1 ($4 - 2 - 1 = 1$).

## 5. The Evaluation Framework: F-Test vs. t-Test

When looking at an OLS summary report (like Python's `statsmodels`), you must read it in a specific order: first check the **F-statistic** to validate the system globally, then check individual **t-statistics**.

### Step 1: Overall Significance (The F-Test)
Before examining individual features, ask: *Is the model doing anything useful at all, or is it just capturing random noise?*

1.  **Hypotheses:**
    * $H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \dots = \beta_k = 0$ (All features are useless; the model is no better than predicting the mean $\bar{y}$).
    * $H_a$: At least one $\beta_i \neq 0$ (At least one feature is meaningful).
2.  **Formula:**
    $$F = \frac{\text{MSR}}{\text{MSE}} = \frac{\text{ESS} / k}{\text{RSS} / (n - k - 1)}$$
    * $\text{MSR}$ = Mean Square Regression (Average Explained Variance per feature).
    * $\text{MSE}$ = Mean Square Error (Average Unexplained Variance per remaining degree of freedom).
3.  **The Intuition:** The F-statistic is an **Explained-to-Unexplained Ratio**. A high F-statistic means the model explains far more variance than random chance. If its corresponding $Prob(\text{F-statistic}) < 0.05$, we reject the null hypothesis and confirm the model is globally valid.

---

### Step 2: Individual Feature Significance (The t-Test)
Once the global model passes the F-test, evaluate each feature (like $X_1$) independently.

1.  **Hypotheses (for feature $X_1$):**
    * $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$ ($X_1$ has zero impact on $Y$).
    * $H_a: \beta_1 \neq 0$ ($X_1$ has a significant relationship with $Y$).
2.  **Formula:**
    $$t = \frac{b_1 - 0}{SE(b_1)}$$
    Where the **Standard Error ($SE$)** formulas measure the dispersion of estimates across samples:
    $$SE(b_1) = \sqrt{\frac{\sum(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{(n - 2)\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2}}$$
    $$SE(b_0) = \sqrt{\frac{\sum(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{n - 2} \left[ \frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2} \right]}$$
3.  **The Intuition:** A high absolute $t$-value shows that the coefficient is many standard deviations away from zero. If $P > |t|$ is less than $0.05$, reject the null hypothesis; the feature is a statistically significant predictor.

---

## 6. Strength of the Fit: $R^2$ vs. Adjusted $R^2$

Once your model proves statistically significant, you measure its explanatory strength.

### $R^2$ Score (Coefficient of Determination)
Tells you the exact percentage of variation in $Y$ that your features explain.
$$R^2 = \frac{\text{ESS}}{\text{TSS}} = 1 - \frac{\text{RSS}}{\text{TSS}}$$
* **Range:** 0 to 1. An $R^2 = 0.897$ means your model accounts for $89.7\%$ of the variance in the target variable.

### The Core Flaw of $R^2$
**$R^2$ can never decrease.** If you keep adding columns to your dataset—even completely useless ones—the math of OLS ensures $R^2$ will stay the same or artificially tick upwards. This easily masks model complexity and encourages overfitting.

### The Solution: Adjusted $R^2$
Adjusted $R^2$ fixes this flaw by adding a penalty for every extra predictor ($k$) you add:
$$\text{Adjusted } R^2 = 1 - \left[ \frac{(1 - R^2)(n - 1)}{n - k - 1} \right]$$

* If you add a highly valuable feature, the jump in $R^2$ easily overcomes the complexity penalty, and Adjusted $R^2$ goes **up**.
* If you add an irrelevant feature, the small increase in $R^2$ cannot offset the penalty, and Adjusted $R^2$ goes **down**.

> ⚠️ **Interview Warning Sign:** Always look closely at your regression output. If there is a **large widening gap** between $R^2$ and Adjusted $R^2$, your model contains irrelevant or highly noisy features that need to be dropped.

---

## 7. Looking at Confidence Intervals

The calculated sample coefficient $b_1$ is only an estimate of the true population $\beta_1$. To convey a realistic range of where the true parameter lies, we create **Confidence Intervals** (usually 95%):
$$CI = b_i \pm \left( t_{\text{critical}} \times SE(b_i) \right)$$

* **Interpretation:** We are $95\%$ confident that the true real-world impact of feature $X_i$ falls within this lower and upper boundary.
* **Crucial Rule:** If your 95% confidence interval range **crosses or contains 0** (e.g., goes from $-0.013$ to $+0.011$), it means a zero-impact relationship is highly plausible. Unsurprisingly, its corresponding p-value will be completely insignificant ($> 0.05$).
