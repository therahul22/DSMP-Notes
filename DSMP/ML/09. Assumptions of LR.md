# Assumptions of Linear Regression: Complete Study Notes

## 1. Linearity (The Baseline Promise)

### I. What is the Assumption?
The model assumes that the true relationship between the independent variables ($X$) and the dependent variable ($Y$) is fundamentally a straight line (or a flat plane). Every unit change in $X$ must cause a constant, proportional shift in $Y$.

### II. What if it Fails?
* **Bias in Parameter Estimates:** The weights ($\beta_i$) the model calculates will be systematically wrong, completely destroying your ability to perform reliable inference.
* **Reduced Predictive Accuracy:** Your model forces a straight line through data that naturally bends, causing it to underfit and miss critical patterns.
* **Invalid Hypothesis Tests:** The violation completely distorts hypothesis tests and confidence intervals, rendering them useless.

### III. How to Detect It?
* **Visual Scatter Plots:** Plot $X$ vs. $Y$ directly. If the relationship appears non-linear, the assumption is broken.
* **Residual Plots:** Plot your residuals ($e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$) against the predicted values ($\hat{y}$). If the assumption holds, you should see a completely random cloud of dots centered around 0 with no discernible pattern. Curvature or systemic trends suggest a violation.
* **Polynomial Terms:** Add a squared term ($X^2$) to your regression. If the new model significantly improves the fit, your underlying relationship was never strictly linear.

### IV. The Remedy
* **Feature Transformations:** Apply math operators to smooth out curves, such as taking $\log(X)$, $\log(Y)$, $\sqrt{X}$, or $1/X$.
* **Polynomial Regression:** Explicitly feed higher-degree features ($X^2, X^3$) into the model to capture the non-linear relationship.
* **Piecewise Regression:** Segment the range of the independent variable and fit separate linear lines to each interval.
* **Non-parametric Methods:** Switch to models that do not rely on linearity, such as Generalized Additive Models (GAMs), splines, or kernel regression.

## 2. Normality of Residuals (The Inferential Engine)



### I. What is the Assumption?
The error terms (residuals) must follow a normal distribution with a mean of zero and a constant variance ($e \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$). OLS does *not* require your input features to be normal, but the errors left behind must form a clean bell curve.

### II. What if it Fails?
* **Inaccurate Hypothesis Tests:** The t-tests (for feature significance) and F-tests (for overall model validity) rely entirely on the math of the normal distribution curve. Non-normal residuals cause these tests to yield incorrect p-values.
* **Invalid Confidence Intervals:** Your calculated margins of error for the regression coefficients will be wrong, affecting the precision of your estimates.
* *Note for interviews:* This assumption is less critical when the sample size is large due to the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), which stabilizes the parameters anyway.

### III. How to Detect It?
* **Visual Histogram:** Plot a histogram of the residuals to visually check for a symmetric, bell-shaped curve.
* **Q-Q (Quantile-Quantile) Plot:** Compares the actual distribution quantiles against standard normal distribution quantiles. If the points fall approximately along a straight diagonal line, the residuals are normal.
* **The Omnibus Test:** A formal statistical test based on the skewness (asymmetry) and kurtosis (tailedness) of the residuals:
  $$K^2 = n \left[ \frac{(\text{skewness})^2}{6} + \frac{(\text{kurtosis})^2}{24} \right]$$
  * **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** Residuals are normally distributed.
  * **Decision:** If the test's p-value is $< 0.05$, reject $H_0$; your residuals are significantly non-normal.

### IV. The Remedy
* **Robust Regression:** Employ techniques like M-estimation or Least Trimmed Squares (LTS) that are heavily resistant to non-normal residual distributions and outliers.
* **Bootstrapping:** Use bootstrap-based inference methods to calculate empirical confidence intervals and tests without relying on normal math curves.
* **Transformations & Non-parametric Models:** Transforming variables or using models like GAMs and splines can alleviate non-normality caused by poor structural fit.

## 3. Homoscedasticity (The Constant Variance Rule)



### I. What is the Assumption?
The spread (variance) of the error terms must remain completely constant across all levels of your independent variables ($\text{Var}(E_i) = \sigma^2$). The error margin cannot expand or contract systematically.

### II. What if it Fails?
* **Heteroscedasticity:** The error variance changes systematically across observations (e.g., variance of spending expanding as income grows).
* **Inefficient Estimates (Loss of BLUE):** While the coefficients remain unbiased, they are no longer the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator. Standard errors become inefficiently large, dampening the statistical power of your tests.
* **Misleading Testing & Margins:** Both your hypothesis tests (t and F tests) and confidence intervals become invalid and highly misleading.

### III. How to Detect It?
* **The Funnel Test (Residual Plot):** Plot the residuals against predicted values or independent features. A random scattering around zero suggests homoscedasticity, while systematic shapes like a funnel indicate heteroscedasticity.
* **The Breusch-Pagan Test:** A formal test modeling whether the variance of your errors is a function of your predictors:
  1. Fit the OLS model and isolate the residuals.
  2. Square those residuals ($e_i^2$).
  3. Regress the squared residuals on your original independent features and grab the resulting $R^2$.
  4. Calculate the Lagrange Multiplier statistic: $LM = n \times R^2$.
  * **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** Error variances are constant (homoscedastic).
  * **Decision:** If the test's p-value is $< 0.05$, reject $H_0$, mathematically proving heteroscedasticity.

### IV. The Remedy
* **Transformations:** Apply logarithmic or square root transformations to variables to stabilize systemic variance.
* **Weighted Least Squares (WLS):** Assign different weights to your observations based on their residual magnitude, down-weighting high-variance observations.
* **Robust Standard Errors:** Keep the OLS coefficients but calculate robust (heteroscedasticity-consistent) standard errors to restore valid hypothesis tests and intervals.

## 4. No Autocorrelation (The Independence Rule)

### I. What is the Assumption?
There should be no apparent correlation or sequential pattern in your residuals. The error terms must be completely independent of one another. This issue is highly common in time-series data.

### II. What if it Fails?
* **Inefficient Estimates:** The parameter coefficients remain unbiased but lose their BLUE status, rendering standard errors unreliable.
* **Inaccurate Tests & Intervals:** The t-tests, F-tests, and confidence intervals become entirely invalid, leading to highly misleading inferences about feature significance.

### III. How to Detect It?
* **The Durbin-Watson Test:** A formal statistical test checking for first-order autocorrelation. The test statistic ranges from 0 to 4:
  * **Value = 2:** Explains perfect independence (No autocorrelation).
  * **Value < 2:** Indicates positive autocorrelation (errors follow each other).
  * **Value > 2:** Indicates negative autocorrelation (errors fluctuate up and down sharply).

### IV. The Remedy
* **Lagged Variables:** Include time-delayed values of the dependent or independent variables as features to capture temporal dependencies.
* **Differencing:** Transform variables into step-by-step changes ($Y_t - Y_{t-1}$) to scrub out absolute baseline correlation.
* **Generalized Least Squares (GLS):** Model with an approach that explicitly incorporates the autocorrelation structure of the errors.
* **Time Series Models:** Transition to models intentionally designed for autocorrelation, like AR, MA, or ARIMA.
* **Robust Standard Errors:** Calculate Newey-West or HAC (Heteroscedasticity and Autocorrelation Consistent) standard errors to correct your inferences.

## 5. No Multicollinearity (The Identity Rule)

### I. What is the Assumption?
Independent variables should show little to no correlation with *each other*. Predictor variables must bring distinct, unique information to the equation.

### II. What if it Fails?
If columns are highly correlated, the underlying matrix math breaks down because the model cannot isolate the independent effect of a single feature. 
* Coefficients become highly volatile, swinging wildly with tiny alterations in training data.
* Standard errors inflate massively, producing large p-values that mask genuinely useful features as statistically insignificant.

### III. How to Detect It?
* **Variance Inflation Factor (VIF):** Run an auxiliary regression predicting each feature against all other remaining features to calculate its structural redundancy:
  $$\text{VIF} = \frac{1}{1 - R_i^2}$$
  * **VIF = 1:** Completely independent.
  * **VIF > 5 to 10:** Severe multicollinearity flag; features are redundant.

### IV. The Remedy
* **Drop a Feature:** Remove one of the highly correlated columns from your model.
* **Feature Engineering:** Combine the collinear metrics into a unified ratio, difference, or index.
* **Dimensionality Reduction:** Run Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to compress your redundant features into uncorrelated, orthogonal components.
* **Regularization:** Shift from classic OLS to penalized techniques like **Ridge** or **Lasso Regression** to stabilize volatile coefficients.
